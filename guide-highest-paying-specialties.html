Highest-Paying Nursing Specialties and How to Get There

Nursing pay depends on education, certification, location and specialty. Here is how the top-earning paths compare and the realistic steps to reach each one.

By the 2Nurses Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Updated September 2026

How nursing pay really varies

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), median pay for registered nurses has recently been in the high-$80,000s to low-$90,000s per year, but the range around that median is wide. Several factors move your earnings more than your job title alone:

  • Education level: Advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs) with graduate degrees generally earn the most.
  • Geography: Pay is often highest in states and metro areas with high costs of living and strong union representation.
  • Setting and shift: Hospitals, outpatient surgery centers and specialty clinics pay differently, and night, weekend and on-call differentials add up.
  • Certification and experience: Many employers pay more for specialty certifications and clinical ladder advancement.

The figures below are approximate, drawn from general BLS occupational data and common market ranges. Actual pay can land well above or below them.

Advanced practice roles

Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist (CRNA)

CRNAs administer anesthesia and monitor patients before, during and after procedures. BLS data consistently places nurse anesthetists at the top of nursing pay, with median earnings above $200,000. The path is long and competitive: a BSN, an RN license, at least one year of full-time critical care experience (many programs prefer more), and a doctoral-level nurse anesthesia program, since accredited programs now award doctoral degrees. After graduating you must pass the national certification exam.

Nurse Practitioner (NP)

NPs diagnose, treat and, depending on state law, prescribe independently or in collaboration with physicians. BLS lists nurse practitioners among the fastest-growing occupations. Specialty affects pay: psychiatric-mental health NPs are often in especially high demand, and acute care, neonatal and some specialty-clinic NPs frequently earn above the NP median. You need a BSN, RN experience (requirements vary by program), an accredited MSN or DNP program in your population focus, and national board certification.

Certified Nurse Midwife (CNM)

CNMs provide prenatal care, attend births and offer gynecologic and primary care. The route is a BSN, RN licensure, a graduate midwifery program and national certification. Pay is generally in line with other APRN roles.

High-paying bedside and non-bedside specialties

You do not need a graduate degree to raise your income. These RN specialties often pay above average, especially with certifications and differentials.

Intensive Care Unit (ICU)

ICU nurses manage ventilators, vasoactive drips and rapidly changing patients. ICU experience is also the gateway to CRNA school. The CCRN certification can boost pay and marketability.

Operating Room (OR)

Perioperative nurses circulate or scrub in surgery. Call pay and specialty teams such as cardiac or neuro surgery can meaningfully increase earnings. The CNOR certification is widely recognized.

Cardiac catheterization lab

Cath lab nurses assist with diagnostic and interventional cardiac procedures. Most labs want ICU, ER or cardiac step-down experience first. On-call pay for emergency procedures is a common income booster.

Labor and delivery (L&D)

L&D nurses manage labor, fetal monitoring and obstetric emergencies. Demand is strong in many regions, and experienced L&D nurses are frequently sought for travel contracts.

Nursing informatics

Informatics nurses optimize electronic health records and clinical workflows. Roles often offer weekday schedules and remote or hybrid options. Clinical experience plus super-user or EHR project work is a typical entry route, with certification and graduate study helping advancement.

Travel nursing

Travel nurses often earn more per week than staff nurses thanks to stipends, particularly in hard-to-fill specialties. Pay fluctuates with demand, so treat it as a strategy rather than a fixed salary.

How to move into a higher-paying specialty

  1. Get your BSN if you have an ADN. Many specialty units and every graduate program require it. Online RN-to-BSN programs let you keep working.
  2. Build acute-care experience. One to two years in med-surg, step-down or ER often opens doors to ICU, OR and procedural areas.
  3. Earn a specialty certification. Certifications demonstrate expertise and often come with a pay differential.
  4. Use internal transfers and fellowships. Many hospitals offer specialty training programs for experienced RNs moving into critical care or perioperative nursing.
  5. Plan graduate school strategically. Compare program outcomes, clinical placement support, cost and accreditation before you apply.
  6. Negotiate. Research local pay ranges before offers and ask about differentials, sign-on bonuses and tuition assistance.

FAQ

What is the highest-paying nursing specialty?

Nurse anesthetist (CRNA) is consistently the highest-paying nursing role in BLS data, with median pay above $200,000. It requires critical care experience and a doctoral-level program.

Can I earn a high salary without a graduate degree?

Yes. Experienced RNs in specialties such as ICU, OR, cath lab and L&D, especially with certifications, overtime, shift differentials or travel contracts, can earn well above the overall RN median.

How long does it take to become a CRNA?

Commonly around seven to ten years in total, including a BSN, at least one year of critical care experience (often more) and a doctoral anesthesia program of roughly three years.

Which nurse practitioner specialty pays the most?

Pay varies by region and setting, but psychiatric-mental health and acute care NP roles are often among the higher-paying NP specialties.
