New Grad Nurse: Your First 90 Days

Passing the boards was the beginning. Here is how to get through orientation, organize your shift and build confidence during your first three months as a registered nurse.

By the 2Nurses Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Updated September 2026

  2. Nurse residency programs

What to expect in your first 90 days

You passed the NCLEX®, accepted your first job and now face the gap between school and real practice. Many new graduates describe the first months as overwhelming, and that is normal. The goal of your first 90 days is not perfection. It is safe practice, steady learning and a support system you can lean on.

A typical first-90-day arc looks like this:

  • Days 1–30: Orientation, learning the unit, shadowing your preceptor and taking one or two patients.
  • Days 31–60: Gradually building to a fuller assignment while your preceptor steps back.
  • Days 61–90: Carrying a full or nearly full assignment, often finishing orientation and working more independently.

Along the way, keep a simple list of skills you have performed and ones you still need to see, such as blood transfusions, chest tube care or admissions. Share it with your preceptor weekly so they can steer the right patients and learning opportunities your way.

Timelines vary by specialty. Med-surg orientations often run around 8 to 12 weeks, while ICU and other specialty units may be longer.

Nurse residency programs

Many hospitals offer nurse residency or transition-to-practice programs for new graduates, commonly lasting around 6 to 12 months. Organizations such as AACN have promoted residencies as a way to improve new nurse confidence and retention.

Residencies typically include:

  • A dedicated preceptor on your unit
  • Regular classroom or simulation sessions on topics like sepsis, rapid responses and communication
  • Peer cohort meetings where new grads share experiences
  • An evidence-based practice or quality improvement project

If you are still job hunting, ask employers about residency length, preceptor consistency, patient ratios during orientation and how they support new grads after orientation ends.

Report sheets that keep you organized

A good report sheet, sometimes called a brain sheet, is your map for the shift. Use your unit's template or build your own, but make sure it captures:

  • Name, age, room, code status, allergies and isolation needs
  • Admitting diagnosis and relevant history
  • Key assessment findings by system
  • Lines, drains, airways and oxygen
  • Scheduled medications and due times
  • Pending labs, imaging and procedures
  • A timeline row for the shift so you can see what is due each hour

Update it throughout the shift, and follow your facility's privacy policies: never take report sheets home, and shred them at the end of the shift.

Communicating with SBAR

SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is a structured communication method widely used in healthcare, including by organizations focused on patient safety. It helps you give clear, concise information when calling a provider or handing off a patient.

StepWhat to sayExample
SituationWho you are, who the patient is, what is happening now"I'm the nurse caring for the patient in room 12. Her blood pressure has dropped."
BackgroundRelevant diagnosis, history and recent changes"She is post-op day one from abdominal surgery."
AssessmentYour findings and what you think is going on"She is dizzy and her urine output is low. I'm concerned about bleeding or fluid loss."
RecommendationWhat you need or suggest"I'd like you to evaluate her. Would you like labs or a fluid bolus?"

Before calling, have the chart, recent vitals, labs, medications and allergies in front of you. Write your SBAR down first. After the call, read back orders and document the conversation.

Time management on a busy shift

  1. Prioritize after report. Identify your sickest or most unstable patient and see them first. Use airway, breathing and circulation plus safety to rank concerns.
  2. Cluster care. Combine assessments, medications and turns in one visit to save steps and reduce interruptions for patients.
  3. Chart as you go. Documenting in real time is safer and prevents a pile-up at the end of the shift.
  4. Delegate appropriately. Know what nursing assistants can do in your state and facility, and communicate clearly when you delegate.
  5. Plan for the unexpected. Admissions, discharges and rapid responses will happen. Leave buffer in your schedule and reassess priorities after every surprise.

Asking for help (and why it is a strength)

The most dangerous new grad is one who never asks questions. Experienced nurses expect you to ask, and doing so protects your patients and your license.

  • Ask when you are unsure, whether about a medication, a policy, a skill or a change in your patient's condition.
  • Know your resources: preceptor, charge nurse, clinical educator, pharmacist, rapid response team and chain of command.
  • Escalate early. If your patient looks worse, call for help before things become an emergency.
  • Take care of yourself. Eat, hydrate and use breaks. If you feel persistently overwhelmed, talk with your educator, manager or employee assistance program.
You do not have to figure it out alone. Connect with a nurse mentor who remembers what the first year felt like.

FAQ

How long is new grad nurse orientation?

It varies by hospital and specialty. Med-surg orientations often run around 8 to 12 weeks, while ICU, ER and other specialty units frequently have longer orientations or residency-based training.

Is it normal to feel overwhelmed as a new nurse?

Yes. Many new graduates find the first months stressful as they transition from school to practice. Support from preceptors, residency cohorts and mentors helps most nurses build confidence over the first year.

What should be on a new grad report sheet?

Include patient identifiers, code status, allergies, diagnosis, assessment findings, lines and drains, medication times, pending tests and an hourly timeline. Follow privacy policies and shred it after the shift.

How do I use SBAR when calling a provider?

State the situation, give brief background, share your assessment and make a clear recommendation or request. Have the chart ready, read back any orders and document the call.
