How to Become a Travel Nurse: Requirements, Pay & Your First Contract

Travel nursing can mean higher pay, new cities and fast skill growth. Here is what you need before you apply, how the money actually works and how to land a strong first assignment.

What travel nursing is (and isn't)

A travel nurse is a registered nurse hired through a staffing agency to fill a temporary gap at a hospital or other facility. Assignments typically run about 13 weeks, although 8-week, 26-week and rolling extensions are common. Facilities use travelers to cover seasonal census spikes, new unit openings, leaves of absence and ongoing vacancies.

You are usually an employee of the agency, not the hospital. The agency negotiates your pay package, handles credentialing and payroll, and often offers benefits such as health insurance and a retirement plan. The facility directs your clinical work day to day.

What travel nursing is not: a shortcut for brand-new graduates, or a job with extensive orientation. Most travelers get a day or two of hospital and unit orientation, then are expected to take a full assignment. That expectation drives nearly every requirement below.

Requirements to become a travel nurse

Requirements vary by agency and facility, but most contracts ask for the following.

1. An active RN license

You need a current, unencumbered RN license, which means graduating from an approved nursing program and passing the NCLEX®-RN. An associate degree (ADN) is often accepted, though some facilities and specialties prefer or require a BSN.

2. Recent clinical experience

Most agencies want at least one year, and often two years, of recent acute-care experience in the specialty you plan to travel in. High-acuity areas such as ICU, ER, labor and delivery, and the OR tend to have the strictest experience expectations.

3. Licensure where you will work

If your primary state of residence belongs to the Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC), a multistate license lets you practice in other compact states. The NCSBN maintains the current list of participating jurisdictions, which has grown to include most states. For non-compact states such as California, you will need to apply for a single-state license by endorsement, which can take weeks, so start early.

4. Certifications and compliance documents

  • BLS for everyone; ACLS for most acute-care units; PALS or NRP for pediatrics and obstetrics
  • Specialty certifications (for example CCRN or CEN) are not always required but make you more competitive
  • Immunization records, TB screening, a physical, a drug screen and a background check
  • Skills checklists and, often, specialty competency exams
  • Two or more recent professional references, ideally from charge nurses or managers

How travel nurse pay works

Travel pay looks different from a staff paycheck. A typical package combines a taxable hourly base rate with non-taxable stipends for housing and for meals and incidentals, plus possible travel reimbursement, completion bonuses and overtime.

ComponentWhat it isWatch for
Taxable base rateHourly wage reported to the IRSA very low base can reduce overtime pay, sick pay and unemployment eligibility
Housing stipendTax-free money for lodging while away from homeOnly tax-free if you maintain a qualifying tax home
Meals & incidentalsTax-free daily allowanceUsually tied to federal GSA per diem rates for the location
Travel reimbursementHelp with getting to and from the assignmentOften paid only after you complete the contract
BonusesSign-on, completion or extension bonusesRead the conditions carefully; cancellations can void them

Because stipends depend on location and demand, weekly gross pay varies widely. Crisis and hard-to-fill assignments pay significantly more than standard contracts, and high-cost cities often carry larger housing stipends. Compare offers by the total weekly amount, the guaranteed hours and the cancellation terms, not just the headline number.

Tax home matters. Tax-free stipends generally require that you keep a permanent residence you pay for and return to while duplicating living expenses on assignment. Rules are nuanced, so talk to a tax professional who works with travel clinicians. You can estimate a package with our travel pay calculator.

How to choose an agency and recruiter

Your recruiter shapes your experience more than almost any other factor. Many travelers work with two or three agencies at once to see more jobs and compare packages.

  • Transparency: Ask for a full pay breakdown in writing, including base rate, stipends and any deductions.
  • Benefits: Check when health insurance starts, whether it continues between contracts and what the retirement match looks like.
  • Guaranteed hours: Some contracts let the facility cancel shifts without pay. Look for a guaranteed-hours clause.
  • Support: Ask who you call at 2 a.m. if there is a problem on the unit or with housing.
  • Joint Commission certification: Many reputable staffing firms hold healthcare staffing certification, which signals a baseline of quality processes.

Landing your first contract, step by step

  1. Build a complete profile. Upload your license, certifications, immunizations and an updated resume that lists unit type, bed count, typical patient ratios, charting system and trauma or teaching status.
  2. Gather references early. Ask your manager and a charge nurse before you resign so they are ready when agencies call.
  3. Be flexible on your first location. Travelers who are open on geography and shift usually land faster. Save your dream city for assignment two or three.
  4. Prepare for the interview. Unit manager interviews are often short phone calls. Have questions ready about ratios, floating policies, orientation, parking and scheduling.
  5. Read the contract line by line. Confirm start date, shift, block scheduling requests, time off, float expectations and cancellation penalties before you sign.
  6. Plan housing. Decide whether to take agency-provided housing or the stipend and find your own. Furnished short-term rentals near hospitals fill quickly.

Thriving in your first weeks on assignment

Arrive early for orientation, learn where supplies and the crash cart live, and find the charge nurse on every shift. Ask how the unit handles rapid responses and which physicians prefer what kind of communication. Keep a small notebook with unit-specific phone numbers and policies.

Expect to float and to receive a heavier assignment than you had at home. Stay within your competency, speak up when an assignment is unsafe and document your concerns through the proper channels. Travelers who are reliable, flexible and kind to the staff are the ones who get offered extensions.

FAQ

Can new graduate nurses become travel nurses?

It is uncommon. Most agencies and hospitals require at least one to two years of recent experience in your specialty because travelers receive very little orientation. New grads usually do best building a solid foundation in a staff role first.

How long is a typical travel nursing contract?

Most contracts run about 13 weeks, but shorter and longer assignments exist. Many travelers extend at a facility they like if the unit still has a need.

Do I need a license in every state I travel to?

You need a license valid in the state where you practice. A multistate compact license covers participating compact states; for other states you apply for a single-state license by endorsement.

Is travel nurse pay really tax-free?

Only the stipend portion can be tax-free, and generally only if you maintain a qualifying tax home and duplicate expenses while on assignment. The hourly base rate is taxable. Consult a qualified tax professional for your situation.
